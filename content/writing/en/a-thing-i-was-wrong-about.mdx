For years I believed that the difference between a serious product and a
side project was the number of hours per week put into it. I built around
this assumption. I judged other people's work by it. I was confidently
wrong, and the change cost me about eighteen months.

## What I had believed

If you wanted a product to grow up, you put more hours into it. Forty if
you must, sixty if you were serious, eighty if you were ambitious. The
hours were the input. The product was the output. The conversion rate
varied by person, but the lever was the same.

## What changed my mind

Two people. The first was a person I admired who shipped, for years, a
small important thing at fifteen hours a week. The product grew slowly
and surely and was widely loved. The other was me, at sixty hours a week,
shipping plenty but shipping the wrong things.

Hours-in did not predict useful-out, in either direction. The conversion
rate was not constant. The hours were not the lever.

## What I think the lever actually is

Whether the work each hour is the thing that needed doing. Not "important
work," not "deep work" — just the actual next thing. Hours spent on the
actual next thing compound. Hours spent on adjacent things degrade.

I built a metric for this for myself, briefly, and then I stopped because
the metric became its own distraction. The honest version is a question I
ask myself at noon: was this morning the actual next thing? Some days
yes. Some days no. The yes days are the ones the product remembers.
